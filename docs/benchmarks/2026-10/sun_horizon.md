# Sun position, sunrise and terrain horizons: validation

GeoSpark's sun functions (`geospark.sun`) and terrain functions (`geospark.visibility`) were compared
with independent references: NREL's Solar Position Algorithm for the Sun, and the European
Commission's PVGIS horizon profiles for terrain. Scripts and results are in
[`sun_horizon/`](sun_horizon/); run October 2026.

## Sun position

**Reference.** NREL's Solar Position Algorithm (Reda and Andreas, 2004), as implemented in pvlib
0.16.1 (`spa_python`, pressure 101,325 Pa, 12 °C). **Cases.** 2,000 random places (latitude −80° to
80°) and times (2000–2050), generated by `make_cases.py` with a fixed seed.

| Quantity | Cases | Median difference | 99th percentile | Maximum |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Apparent elevation, Sun above 5° | 903 | 0.002° | 0.011° | 0.014° |
| Azimuth, Sun above 5° | 903 | 0.003° | 0.022° | 0.21° ¹ |
| Apparent elevation, Sun between −1° and 5° | 131 | 0.004° | 0.37° | 0.38° ² |

¹ The largest azimuth differences occur with the Sun within 2° of the zenith, where azimuth is
ill-conditioned. ² The largest near-horizon differences occur with the Sun just below the horizon
(−0.9°), where the two refraction models differ; above the horizon the agreement is as in the first
row.

## Sunrise and sunset

**Reference.** The second at which the true elevation computed by NREL's SPA crosses −0.8333°
(upper limb on a flat horizon with standard refraction), found by sampling every second over 36
hours (`brute_all.py`). **Cases.** 300 random days in 2026 at latitudes −60° to 60°; 899 events.

| | Events | Median | 99th percentile | Maximum |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Difference, seconds | 899 | 1.1 s | 3.9 s | 4.7 s |

pvlib's `sun_rise_set_transit_spa` was used first and disagreed by up to 2.5 minutes at some
longitudes. Against the brute-force crossings, GeoSpark agrees to the second and that routine does
not: it selects events by UTC day, so an event after midnight UTC belongs to another day's
calculation. The brute-force crossings are therefore the reference reported above.

## Terrain horizon

**Reference.** PVGIS 5.3 `printhorizon` (European Commission Joint Research Centre), which computes
the horizon from its own DEM at 7.5° spacing. GeoSpark computed the horizon from Copernicus GLO-30 at
the same azimuths, observer at ground level, out to 30 km (`pvgis_cmp.py`).

| Site | Mean difference | Mean absolute difference | Maximum absolute difference | Correlation |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Innsbruck, Austria | +0.06° | 0.21° | 1.78° | 0.998 |
| Bright, Victoria | +0.15° | 0.52° | 2.94° | 0.941 |
| Chamonix, France | −0.06° | 0.56° | 2.40° | 0.995 |
| Queenstown, New Zealand | +0.30° | 0.60° | 2.19° | 0.990 |
| Hobart, Tasmania | +0.63° | 0.70° | 4.43° | 0.851 |
| Marysville, Victoria | +0.23° | 0.89° | 2.40° | 0.833 |
| Cape Town, South Africa | +1.03° | 1.48° | 4.73° | 0.932 |
| Lauterbrunnen, Switzerland | −1.59° | 3.05° | 10.89° | 0.945 |

At six of the eight sites the mean absolute difference is below 1°. The two larger differences
have identifiable causes: Cape Town is an urban site, where the Copernicus surface model includes
buildings and its horizon is consistently higher (+1.03° on average); Lauterbrunnen lies in a
glacial valley with near-vertical walls a few hundred metres away, where the horizon angle changes
by several degrees over one DEM cell, and the two DEMs differ in resolution and in their position of
the cliff edges. A horizon in such terrain should be treated as uncertain by several degrees.

Reading distant terrain (beyond 5 km) from the DEM's ~120 m overviews instead of the full 30 m grid
changed the Bright horizon by at most 0.15° (median 0°), and is used by default.

## Line of sight

Line of sight and Fresnel clearance are tested on synthetic terrain with known answers
(`tests/test_sun_visibility.py`): a ridge of known height at a known distance, and the Earth bulge
over 60 km of flat ground (61 m at mid-path with k = 0.13). No independent real-world reference
has been compared yet.

## Limitations

- Copernicus GLO-30 is a surface model at about 30 m: forest canopy and large buildings are
  included coarsely and anything narrower than a cell is not. An observer placed on the DEM
  surface stands on a roof or the canopy where there is one; `ground_elevation_m` overrides it.
- Refraction uses the standard coefficient k = 0.13 for terrain and Bennett's formula for the Sun
  at the skyline; real refraction varies with the weather, most near the horizon.
- Sunrise and sunset are for a flat horizon; hours of direct sun with `terrain=True` use the
  terrain horizon, sampled every 2 minutes.
